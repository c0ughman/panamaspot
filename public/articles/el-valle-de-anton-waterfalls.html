El Valle de Antón sits inside the eroded crater of an extinct volcano, and the rivers draining its forested walls drop fast before leveling out on the caldera floor. That geology produces a cluster of waterfalls within a few kilometers of each other — some ticketed and polished, some free and genuinely wild. This guide covers all of them: what they actually look like, what they cost, whether you can swim, and which ones are worth your limited time.

No other guide on the internet resolves the conflicting height figures, ranks every fall by difficulty, or gives you a realistic safety briefing for Chorro Las Mozas. This one does all three. For a deep dive on El Valle's flagship cascade, see our full Chorro El Macho guide. For everything else the valley offers, the El Valle de Antón things-to-do hub is your starting point.

Why the waterfalls matter

Why the waterfalls of El Valle de Antón matter

Most crater towns in Central America have one waterfall worth visiting. El Valle has at least five, spread around the caldera rim at different elevations, with different characters. Chorro El Macho is the crowd-pleaser: easy trail, zipline, swimming pool, done. Chorro Las Mozas is the local's spot: free entry, natural swimming holes, slippery rocks, no guardrails. Lovers Falls rewards a short uphill scramble with a series of cascading pools. Chorro El Escondido sits on the La India Dormida trail and catches almost nobody. Pozo Azul, technically just outside the caldera, is the one that will genuinely stop your breath. For more falls across the country, Panama's best waterfalls covers routes, hike times and entry fees.

The falls are also spread across the crater in a way that makes multi-stop days logical. El Macho is on the northern road toward La Mesa. Las Mozas is southwest of the town square along the Río Antón. Lovers Falls is on the eastern side of the valley. That spread — roughly 3 to 5 kilometers between any two points — is exactly why getting around on two wheels makes sense. An e-bike from E-Valley Bikes covers the distance between El Macho and Las Mozas in under 15 minutes each way, leaving you maximum time at the water rather than on the road.

Field note · Logistics

All of El Valle's waterfalls are accessible without a car. El Macho and Las Mozas can be walked from the town square in 25–35 minutes. Lovers Falls and El Escondido require more effort on foot — a taxi ($3–5 one way) or a rental bike saves your legs for the actual trails.

The height debate settled

The height debate: 35 meters or 70 meters?

Search for Chorro El Macho and you will find two figures: 35 meters and 70 meters. Both appear on legitimate-looking websites. Neither source explains the discrepancy. Here is the answer.

The main cascade — the single curtain of white water visible from the suspension bridges and the zipline platform — drops approximately 35 meters (around 115 feet). This is the figure used by TripAdvisor, Lonely Planet, and every travel writer who has measured or cited a primary source. It is the height of roughly a ten-story building, which is already impressive. The 70-meter figure that appears on some local tourism sites and the official el-valle-panama.com page refers to the total vertical drop of the Río Antón through the entire gorge section, including a series of smaller cascades above and below the main falls. When guides and locals say "Chorro El Macho," they mean the single main drop. That drop is 35 meters. The 70-meter claim is not fabricated — it is simply measuring a different thing without saying so.

Does the distinction matter on the ground? Not really. At peak wet-season flow, the volume of water coming over that 35-meter drop is substantial enough to drench anyone standing on the lower viewing platform. In the dry season (January through April), the same falls shrink to a narrower ribbon. Both versions are worth seeing; they just feel like different waterfalls.

Field note · Settled

Chorro El Macho's main cascade is 35 meters. The 70-meter figure refers to the full gorge system. Expect the 35-meter drop to look dramatically different between dry season (thin ribbon) and peak rainy season (thundering curtain).

Chorro El Macho — the flagship

Chorro El Macho is the most-visited waterfall in El Valle de Antón, and for good reason: the trail is short, the setting is beautiful, and the zipline that crosses directly in front of the falls is one of the most photographed adventure experiences in central Panama. The eco-park entrance is approximately 2 kilometers north of the town square on the road toward La Mesa. GPS: approximately 8.6195° N, 80.1285° W.

Entry fee: $5 per person for trail access and the natural plunge pool viewpoint. Swimming in the separate man-made pool fed by the river costs an additional $5. The zipline (Canopy Adventure) runs around $53 per person and includes two passes directly in front of the falls — confirm current pricing at the gate, as it changes seasonally. Hours are 8:00 am to 5:00 pm daily.

The trail: A 200-meter paved path leads from the ticket booth to the main viewpoint. From there, wooden suspension bridges cross the gorge and give you close-up views of the cascade from multiple angles. The full loop, including the bridges and the swimming pool area, takes 45 minutes at a relaxed pace. The trail is suitable for families with children and requires no hiking experience. For longer routes, the best hiking trails in Panama lists distances, times and how to reach each trailhead.

Swimming: The natural plunge pool at the base of the falls is cold, clear, and genuinely refreshing — included in the base $5 entry. The man-made pool nearby is warmer and calmer, better for young children, but costs extra. Neither pool has lifeguards on duty, so standard water-safety awareness applies.

What makes it worth it: The combination of easy access, dramatic scenery, and the zipline option makes El Macho the right choice for first-time visitors, families, and anyone with limited time. If you have only one waterfall slot in your itinerary, this is it. Chorro Las Mozas — the local's waterfall

Chorro Las Mozas is the waterfall that El Valle residents actually visit on weekends. It is free (entry is $1 for adults, $0.75 for seniors, $0.50 for children — a nominal conservation fee collected at the trailhead), genuinely wild, and requires more physical effort than El Macho. It is also the most dangerous waterfall in the valley, and that needs to be said plainly before the directions.

Getting there: From the town square, head southwest along the Río Antón. The trailhead is approximately 2 kilometers from the center — a 25-minute walk or a 5-minute taxi ride ($2–3). The path follows the right bank of the river. GPS for the trailhead area: approximately 8.5980° N, 80.1420° W. In the dry season the path is clearly visible. In the rainy season (May through November), vegetation closes in significantly and the trail becomes harder to follow — go with someone who knows it, or ask at your hostel for a local guide.

What you find: The Río Antón drops through a series of natural pools and small cascades before reaching the main swimming hole at Chorro Las Mozas — a compact waterfall feeding a beautiful natural pool that is perfect for swimming and hanging out. Local youth use the upper pools for cliff jumping, which looks spectacular and carries real risk (more on that below). The pools are cold, clear, and surrounded by dense vegetation. On weekday mornings you may have the place nearly to yourself. On weekend afternoons in the rainy season it fills with Panamanian families.

Difficulty: Moderate to strenuous, depending on conditions. The approach trail is easy. The final section to the main pool requires scrambling along slippery rock faces at the water's edge. There are no ropes, no handrails, and no marked route — you pick your way along narrow ledges above the river. In the rainy season, the rocks are consistently wet and the water level rises, making the scramble significantly more hazardous.

Safety · Las Mozas

Wear closed-toe shoes with grip — not flip-flops, not Crocs. The rock face leading to the main pool is genuinely slippery and a fall onto the rocks below is a serious injury risk. The pools contain submerged rocks and hidden cave formations beneath the surface. Do not jump into any pool without first checking depth. There are no lifeguards and no rescue infrastructure on site.

Lovers Falls (Cascadas de los Enamorados)

Lovers Falls — known locally as Cascadas de los Enamorados — is El Valle's most underrated waterfall. TripAdvisor users consistently rate it higher than El Macho for natural beauty (4.3 versus 3.7 as of 2026), yet it draws a fraction of the visitors. The reason is simple: getting there requires a 30-minute uphill hike on a rocky trail, and most people don't bother.

Getting there: The trailhead is on the eastern side of the valley, accessible by taxi from the town center ($3–4 one way). From the drop-off point, the trail climbs steadily through secondary forest for approximately 1.5 kilometers before descending to the falls. The path is not always clearly marked — ask your driver or accommodation for current conditions, as trail maintenance is inconsistent.

What you find: Not a single drop but a series of cascading pools stepping down through the forest. The upper pools are shallow and good for sitting in; the lower main pool is deeper and suitable for swimming. The surrounding vegetation is denser than at El Macho, and the falls feel genuinely secluded even when other visitors are present. There is no entry fee, no ticket booth, and no infrastructure — bring your own water and snacks.

Difficulty: Moderate. The uphill section is steep in places but manageable for anyone in reasonable fitness. Wet-season conditions make the descent to the pools slippery. Hiking boots or trail shoes are strongly recommended.

Best for: Travelers who want a more adventurous experience without the full commitment of a technical hike. The combination of multiple pools, no crowds, and no entrance fee makes Lovers Falls the best value waterfall in the valley.

Chorro El Escondido — the hidden fall

Chorro El Escondido ("the hidden waterfall") sits on the La India Dormida trail, roughly 20 minutes from the trailhead. Most hikers walk straight past it, focused on reaching the ridge. That oversight is their loss.

Getting there: The La India Dormida trailhead is on the western edge of town, a 15-minute walk from the square. The entry fee for the La India Dormida trail is $3. Chorro El Escondido appears on the left side of the trail before the main ascent steepens — listen for the water. It is easy to miss if you are moving fast.

What you find: A narrow cascade dropping into a small, shallow pool surrounded by mossy rocks and ferns. It is not a swimming hole — the pool is too shallow and too small. But it is genuinely beautiful in a quiet, intimate way that the larger falls cannot replicate. In the dry season it shrinks to a trickle; in the rainy season it runs full and loud enough to hear from the trail above.

Best for: Hikers already doing La India Dormida who want to add a waterfall stop without extra cost or effort. You can visit El Escondido and complete the ridge hike in the same morning. See our El Valle hiking guide for the full La India Dormida trail breakdown.

Pozo Azul — worth the extra distance

Pozo Azul is not technically inside the El Valle caldera — it sits in the Coclé countryside roughly 30 to 40 minutes by road from the town center — but it belongs in any serious El Valle waterfall guide because it is genuinely extraordinary and almost no international visitors know about it.

What you find: A series of waterfalls cascading down through a canyon into crystal-clear azure pools — the name means "blue pool" and the color, caused by mineral content in the water, is real and not a filter. The pools are deep enough for cliff jumping (practiced by locals), and the canyon walls create a dramatic setting that feels more like a scene from a nature documentary than a tourist attraction. Rock scrambling between pools is part of the experience.

Getting there: You need your own transport or a hired taxi for the day ($20–30 round trip from El Valle, including wait time). There is no direct bus. The road to Pozo Azul is paved for most of the route but narrows toward the end — a standard sedan can manage it in dry conditions. In the rainy season, check road conditions before going.

Entry fee: Minimal or none — the site is on private farmland and a small donation to the landowner is customary ($1–2 per person). Conditions change; ask locally before you go.

Difficulty: Moderate to strenuous. Getting between the pools requires scrambling over wet rocks. The cliff jumps are for experienced swimmers only — depths vary and submerged rocks exist. Do not jump without local guidance on safe entry points.

Field note · Pozo Azul

If you have a rental car or are willing to hire a taxi for a half-day, Pozo Azul is the single most spectacular natural swimming spot within reach of El Valle. Go on a weekday morning to avoid weekend crowds from Penonomé and Panama City.

Best season for El Valle de Antón waterfalls

El Valle has two distinct seasons and they produce dramatically different waterfall experiences. Understanding the difference is the single most useful thing you can know before planning your visit.

Rainy season (May through November): This is when the waterfalls are at their best. El Macho runs as a full, thundering curtain of white water. Las Mozas fills its pools to swimmable depth. Lovers Falls runs multiple cascades simultaneously. Chorro El Escondido is audible from the trail above. The trade-off is that afternoon rain is almost guaranteed, trails become slippery, and Las Mozas in particular becomes significantly more hazardous. Go in the morning — by 9:00 am the skies are usually clear, and most afternoon rains arrive between 2:00 and 4:00 pm.

Dry season (December through April): Water levels drop substantially. El Macho shrinks from a curtain to a ribbon — still photogenic, but less dramatic. Las Mozas may be too shallow for comfortable swimming in February and March. Lovers Falls and El Escondido are at their least impressive. The upside: trails are dry, the scrambles at Las Mozas are safer, and the overall hiking experience is more comfortable. January and February are the driest months.

Month-by-month quick guide:

  • January–February: Dry, low water. Best for hiking; waterfalls at minimum flow. Las Mozas safe but shallow.
  • March–April: Still dry. El Macho at its least impressive. Good for Pozo Azul (road accessible).
  • May–June: Rains begin. Water levels rise quickly. Good balance of flow and manageable conditions.
  • July–August: Peak flow. El Macho at its most dramatic. Las Mozas pools full. Go early.
  • September–October: Heaviest rains. Waterfalls spectacular but Las Mozas trail can flood. Pozo Azul road may be difficult.
  • November: Rains taper. Still good flow. One of the best months overall.
  • December: Transition. Water still reasonable. Drier conditions returning.
Field note · Timing

The sweet spot for El Valle waterfalls is May through June and October through November — enough rain to fill the falls, not so much that trails become genuinely dangerous. If you are visiting in the dry season, prioritize El Macho (the infrastructure makes it worthwhile regardless of flow) and Pozo Azul (the blue pools are striking year-round).

Safety guide for El Valle waterfalls

Most waterfall injuries in El Valle happen at Chorro Las Mozas, and most of those involve slipping on wet rock. The following applies to all the valley's natural falls, but Las Mozas in particular.

Footwear: Closed-toe shoes with rubber grip are non-negotiable at Las Mozas and Lovers Falls. Trail runners work well. Hiking boots are better. Flip-flops and Crocs are genuinely dangerous — the rock surfaces at Las Mozas are consistently wet and often coated in algae. At El Macho, the paved trail is fine in any footwear, but the natural pool area has slippery rocks at the water's edge.

Jumping: Local youth jump from the upper ledges at Las Mozas into the pools below. This looks exciting and is genuinely dangerous. The pools contain submerged rocks and hidden cave formations beneath the surface. Water depth varies with season. Do not jump into any pool at any El Valle waterfall without first checking the depth yourself by entering from a safe access point. At Pozo Azul, local guides know the safe jump spots — follow their lead.

Water levels: In the rainy season, water levels at Las Mozas can rise fast after heavy rain. If the river looks significantly higher than normal or the current in the pools is strong, do not attempt the scramble to the main pool. The same applies to Pozo Azul — the canyon can flood rapidly.

Solo hiking: Do not attempt Las Mozas alone, especially in the rainy season. The trail is not marked, the rock scramble is genuinely hazardous, and there is no rescue infrastructure. Go with at least one other person, or hire a local guide ($10–15 for a half-day) through your accommodation.

Medical access: El Valle has a basic health center (Centro de Salud) on the main road near the town square. For anything serious, the nearest hospital is in Penonomé, approximately 45 minutes by road. This is not a reason to avoid the waterfalls — it is a reason to take the safety guidance above seriously.

Photography tips for El Valle de Antón waterfalls

Every waterfall in El Valle photographs differently, and the best results come from knowing when and where to stand before you arrive.

Chorro El Macho: The best angle is from the lower suspension bridge, which puts you at eye level with the mid-section of the falls and gives you the full drop in frame. Shoot in the morning (8:00–10:00 am) when the light comes over the eastern crater rim and hits the water directly. By midday the falls are in shadow from the surrounding forest canopy. Use a slow shutter speed (1/15s to 1/4s) to get silky water movement — a small tripod or monopod helps on the bridge. The zipline passes directly in front of the falls and makes for a dramatic action shot if you time it right; ask the operators when the next run is.

Chorro Las Mozas: The main pool photographs best from the rock ledge on the far side of the pool, which gives you the falls as a backdrop with the pool in the foreground. Morning light is again preferable — the canopy is dense and afternoon light is flat. Waterproof your camera or phone before the scramble; the rocks are wet and a drop into the river is a real possibility.

Lovers Falls: The series of cascading pools photographs well from above — find the highest accessible ledge and shoot down into the pools for a perspective that shows the step-down structure of the falls. The surrounding vegetation is dense enough to frame the shot naturally without needing a wide angle.

Pozo Azul: The blue color of the pools is most vivid in direct overhead sunlight (11:00 am to 1:00 pm), which is the opposite of most waterfall photography advice. The mineral color is the story here, so prioritize midday light over soft morning light. A polarizing filter cuts surface glare and makes the blue significantly more saturated.

General: All El Valle waterfalls are in dense forest and benefit from overcast skies, which eliminate harsh shadows and even out the contrast between white water and dark rock. Rainy-season mornings — after overnight rain but before the afternoon clouds build — often produce the best combination of full water flow and soft, even light.

One-morning itinerary

One-morning waterfall itinerary

The two most accessible and complementary waterfalls — Chorro El Macho and Chorro Las Mozas — are on opposite sides of the valley, roughly 4 kilometers apart. Hitting both in a single morning is entirely doable if you start early and move efficiently.

7:30 am: Leave your accommodation and head to Chorro Las Mozas first. Going early means you beat the weekend crowds and have the best chance of dry trail conditions before the morning humidity builds. The $1 entry fee is collected at the trailhead. Allow 90 minutes: 25 minutes in, 30 minutes at the pools, 25 minutes back, plus buffer for the rock scramble.

9:30 am: Head to Chorro El Macho. From the Las Mozas trailhead, it is approximately 4 kilometers to the El Macho entrance — a 15-minute taxi ride ($3–4) or a pleasant e-bike ride through the valley floor. The morning light is still good for photography at El Macho until around 10:30 am. Allow 60–90 minutes including the suspension bridge loop and a swim in the natural pool.

11:30 am: Back in town for lunch. The Sunday market near the town square is the best food stop if you are visiting on a weekend. On other days, the fondas along Avenida Central serve solid rice, chicken, and patacones for $3–5.

The e-bike option is worth flagging here: the road between Las Mozas and El Macho passes through the flat caldera floor and is straightforward cycling terrain. E-Valley Bikes rents electric bikes from $35 for 2 hours, with a helmet and gear included — the assist makes the slight uphill toward El Macho effortless and leaves your legs fresh for the actual waterfall scrambles. It is also the most enjoyable way to move through the valley between stops, with views of the crater rim visible in every direction.

Field note · Itinerary

Start at Las Mozas, finish at El Macho. Las Mozas rewards early arrivals with empty pools and better trail conditions. El Macho's infrastructure means timing matters less — the site is well-managed regardless of when you arrive. Never reverse this order on a weekend; Las Mozas fills with locals by mid-morning.

Questions

Before you book

How tall is Chorro El Macho waterfall?

The main cascade drops 35 meters (approximately 115 feet). The 70-meter figure that appears on some websites refers to the total vertical drop of the Río Antón through the entire gorge system, including smaller cascades above and below the main falls. The single drop you see from the viewing platform and suspension bridges is 35 meters.

Is Chorro Las Mozas free to enter?

Almost. There is a nominal conservation fee of $1 for adults, $0.75 for seniors, and $0.50 for children, collected at the trailhead. This is far cheaper than El Macho's $5 entry and the experience is more adventurous, though also more demanding physically.

Can you swim at Chorro El Macho?

Yes. The natural plunge pool at the base of the falls is included in the standard $5 entry fee. There is also a separate man-made pool fed by the river, which costs an additional $5 and is calmer — better for young children. Neither pool has lifeguards on duty.

What is the best time of year to visit El Valle de Antón waterfalls?

May through November (rainy season) produces the fullest, most dramatic waterfalls. The sweet spot is May–June and October–November, when rainfall is consistent but not extreme. In the dry season (January–April), water levels drop significantly — El Macho shrinks to a ribbon and Las Mozas may be too shallow for comfortable swimming.

Is Chorro Las Mozas dangerous?

It requires genuine caution. The final approach to the main pool involves scrambling along wet, algae-covered rock ledges with no handrails or marked route. Slipping is a real risk. Wear closed-toe shoes with rubber grip, never flip-flops. The pools contain submerged rocks and hidden cave formations — do not jump in without checking depth first. Do not attempt it alone, especially in the rainy season.

How do I get to Chorro El Macho from El Valle town center?

The eco-park entrance is approximately 2 kilometers north of the town square on the road toward La Mesa. You can walk in 25–35 minutes, take a taxi for $3–5 one way, or rent a bike. The GPS coordinates for the entrance are approximately 8.6195° N, 80.1285° W.

Are there waterfalls in El Valle suitable for children?

Yes. Chorro El Macho is the best option for families — the trail is paved and short, the suspension bridges are safe and exciting for kids, and the man-made swimming pool is calm and shallow. Lovers Falls is manageable for older children with hiking experience. Chorro Las Mozas is not recommended for young children due to the slippery rock scramble.

What is Pozo Azul and is it worth visiting from El Valle?

Pozo Azul is a series of waterfalls cascading into mineral-blue pools in the Coclé countryside, about 30–40 minutes by road from El Valle. It is arguably the most spectacular natural swimming spot within reach of the valley and is largely unknown to international visitors. You need your own transport or a hired taxi ($20–30 round trip). It is worth the effort, particularly on a weekday when crowds are minimal.

Do I need a guide for El Valle waterfalls?

El Macho requires no guide — the site is well-managed with clear signage. For Las Mozas, a local guide ($10–15 for a half-day) is strongly recommended in the rainy season when the trail is harder to follow and conditions are more hazardous. For Lovers Falls and Pozo Azul, a guide is helpful but not essential if you have good navigation skills and appropriate footwear.

What is the best way to visit multiple waterfalls in one day?

Start at Chorro Las Mozas early (7:30 am) to beat crowds and get the best trail conditions, then move to Chorro El Macho by mid-morning. The two sites are about 4 kilometers apart — a 15-minute taxi ride or a pleasant e-bike ride through the valley floor. E-Valley Bikes rents electric bikes from $35 for 2 hours, which makes the inter-waterfall transit easy and enjoyable.

What should I bring to El Valle de Antón waterfalls?

Closed-toe shoes with grip (essential for Las Mozas and Lovers Falls), a change of clothes and a dry bag for your phone and valuables, water (at least 1.5 liters per person), sunscreen, and cash for entry fees. A small first-aid kit is sensible for the more adventurous falls. Bring a waterproof phone case or a separate waterproof camera if you plan to photograph near the water.

Is there a combo ticket for multiple El Valle waterfalls?

No combined ticket exists across the different sites. El Macho is privately managed and sells its own entry. Las Mozas has a separate nominal fee. Lovers Falls and El Escondido are free (El Escondido's $3 fee covers the La India Dormida trail). Budget approximately $10–15 per person to cover entry fees for a full waterfall day, excluding transport.
