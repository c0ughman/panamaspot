Three corrections before you start

I re-measured everything against the live files to build this queue. Two numbers in the audit were wrong and one thing I missed turns out to be the cheapest win on the list.

Correction 1 — title lengths

The audit said 8 pages have titles over 60 characters. The real number is 1. I was counting the HTML entity &amp; as five characters instead of the one ampersand Google actually renders. Only /articles/hikes-el-valle-de-anton genuinely truncates, at 62 characters. Don't spend time on this.

Correction 2 — description lengths

Same entity bug. The audit said 13 pages have descriptions over 160 characters; the real number is 6, carrying 3,389 impressions. Still worth fixing, but it is a twenty-minute job, not an afternoon.

New finding — and it is the best effort-to-impact item on the list

Your footer renders its entire Destinations, By topic and About columns as <span class="footer-link-muted"> — plain text, not links. That is 18 dead links per page across all 48 pages: 864 internal links that render but do nothing.

Three of those destinations already have enough articles behind them to justify a hub (Panama City 14, Boquete & Chiriquí 27, Bocas del Toro 3). Turning those into real links is a fifteen-minute script that raises sitewide internal linking more than any other single change available to you. The other four (San Blas, Azuero, Coiba, Darién) should stay unlinked until you have the content — which is exactly what section 05 covers.

06

Your monetisation model — what I think

You described it as: build content around high-ticket, decent-volume keywords, establish position, then sell direct deals to vendors in that niche rather than running affiliate. Here is my honest read.

The core call is right, and it is right for a specific reason worth naming

Direct vendor deals beat affiliate at your scale by a margin that is not close. At 397 clicks a month, travel affiliate on a 4–8% commission with a 2–3% booking conversion would produce something like $10 to $40 a month. Your existing El Valley Bikes deal pays $500. That is not a 10× difference, it is closer to 25×, and it holds until you are somewhere north of 15,000–20,000 clicks a month. You will not cross that line for at least a year, so affiliate is not a decision you need to revisit in 2026.

The deeper reason it is right: affiliate pays you for transactions, and you have no transaction infrastructure and no way to influence conversion once the click leaves. Direct deals pay you for attention, which is the thing you actually produce and the thing you can prove.

But you have a proof problem, and it is costing you money right now

You have three outbound links to El Valley Bikes across 48 articles and zero click tracking on any of them. Today, if that client asks what they got for their $500, your honest answer is that you don't know. And when you go to sell the second deal, your pitch is a story rather than a number.

This is the single highest-return thing in this entire document relative to effort. Thirty minutes of work — tagged outbound links, a click counter, a monthly figure — and your next pitch changes from "I'll build you traffic" to "last month I sent 340 people who were actively searching for bike tours in El Valle to a competitor, because you weren't there." That second sentence sells itself and it prices itself much higher than $500. Do this today, in Block D, before you write a single new article.

Where I'd push back: your niche-selection criteria are one factor short

You named high ticket and decent volume. Both correct. The missing third criterion is supplier fragmentation — how many businesses could plausibly buy the same asset from you.

Look at your own examples through that lens. Yacht charters and villa rentals fail it — high ticket, yes, but supply is thin, the operators are few, and the search results are owned by Booking, Airbnb and specialist charter platforms with authority you will not touch in two years. You would build the content and then find there are four possible buyers and you cannot rank anyway.

Multi-day boat tours pass it easily. San Blas sailing, Coiba liveaboards, Bocas island-hopping charters: ticket sizes of $400–$1,500, dozens of small independent operators with no marketing function, and genuinely thin content competition because nobody has written the comparison page properly. If one churns you re-sell the same asset next week. That is the shape you want.

Also worth adding to the list: coffee-farm tours in Boquete (high margin, many operators, and you already rank at position 11 with 1,540 impressions on that exact topic) and Volcán Barú summit trips (guides are required, fragmented, and you rank position 9 there already).

The risk you should be sizing

One client is 100% of your revenue on this property, and the deal is flat-rate, which means if the traffic triples your income does not change. That is fine as a first deal — you needed the proof case — but the structure to move toward is a floor plus a variable component, or simply a higher flat rate renegotiated once you can show the click numbers. Get the tracking live, run it sixty days, then have that conversation with data in hand.

07

Backlinks — do they make sense here?

Short answer: yes, and your data shows it unusually clearly. But there is a prerequisite you are currently failing, and the realistic ceiling is smaller than most people selling link-building will tell you.

The evidence, straight from your own numbers

Look at the split. Your pages rank position 5–15 on long-tail queries and position 47–76 on head terms — panama city 53, el valle de anton 47, boquete 60, bocas islands 76. Same site, same pages, same 8,000-word depth, same clean schema.

When content quality is constant and the only thing that changes is competition level, the variable separating those two groups is domain authority. Long-tail is decided by relevance, which you win. Head terms are decided by authority, which you do not have. That is as clean a diagnostic signature as this data produces, and it says links are a genuine constraint — not a nice-to-have.

Do this before you send a single outreach email

Your site has no About page, no Contact page, no author pages and no privacy policy. The only pages that exist are the home page, /es, and 48 articles. Your own footer advertises Our writers, Editorial standards, Work with us, Press & partners and Contact — all of them dead text.

Nearly every directory worth being in requires a contact page. No tourism board links to a site with no named humans. And your footer currently claims the site is "written and edited by people who live in Panama" with zero attribution anywhere to support it — which is both an outreach blocker and an E-E-A-T liability. This is two hours of work and it gates the entire channel. It is in Block D for that reason.

The easiest links — get these this week

  1. Your own clients and the operators you already write about.Highest conversion rate of anything on this list. You send El Valley Bikes traffic for free right now — a "recommended local guide" link on their site costs you one email. Repeat for every coffee farm, hot spring and tour operator you have covered favourably.
  2. Panamanian expat community sites and forums.Chiriqui.life, Boquete community boards, Panama expat Facebook groups with resource pages, r/panama. These are genuinely useful placements — your practical-logistics pages are exactly what those audiences ask for weekly.
  3. Regional tourism and chamber listings.Autoridad de Turismo de Panamá business directory, Cámara de Turismo de Chiriquí, municipal tourism pages for Antón and Boquete. Free, slow to approve, and they carry real local relevance signals.
  4. Wikivoyage and OpenStreetMap-adjacent resources.Wikivoyage external links are achievable if the page is genuinely the best reference for a specific attraction. Wikipedia is not worth chasing.

The best links — worth real effort

  1. A linkable data asset.The highest-leverage thing you could build: month-by-month rainfall and trail-condition data by region, or Volcán Barú summit sunrise times and weather. Original, updated, genuinely useful, and it earns links passively forever instead of one at a time. This is what separates sites that stall at 50 referring domains from ones that don't.
  2. Panamanian press travel desks.La Prensa, TVN, Panamá América. Pitch a data story, not your site — "we analysed what tourists actually search for about Panama" is a story a travel desk will run, and it comes with a citation.
  3. Hotels and lodges as partners, not targets.Boquete and El Valle lodges maintain "what to do nearby" pages and update them badly. Offer them a genuinely good, maintained resource for their guests. Their domains are older and more trusted than any blog you could pitch.
  4. The Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute orbit.STRI is headquartered in Panama and the .edu/.org ecosystem around Coiba, quetzal season and Barú birdlife is real. A rigorously researched, properly cited piece on quetzal season is the kind of thing that picks up academic and conservation citations. Slow, but these are the links that actually move head terms.

On paying for links, since you asked directly

Buying links violates Google's spam policies and the enforcement risk is real, so I'll give you the practical version rather than a lecture. Paid guest posts on generic travel blogs are close to worthless — those sites sell to everyone, the neighbourhoods are bad, and you are paying for a signal Google has been discounting for a decade. If you spend money, spend it on placements that would drive referral traffic even if the link were nofollow: a sponsorship of a Panama expat newsletter, a listing on a genuinely-used tourism directory, a partnership with a lodge. Those survive any algorithm update because they are not really link building. Never buy a package priced per link.

My actual recommendation on sequencing

Spend two hours this week on the easy tier — the operator emails alone are worth more than most people's month of outreach, because you have genuine leverage with those businesses. Build the About and Contact pages as part of that.

But do not run a serious link campaign yet. Links multiply the surface area you already have, and right now that surface is 48 pages. The same campaign run after the 36 new pages land is worth roughly twice as much, because it lifts 84 pages instead of 48. Content first, then links, then the vendor pitch. That order is not arbitrary — each one makes the next one cheaper.
